China Furniture Sourcing
Factories, Materials, Costs

Furniture is the hardest category to buy from China and the easiest to get wrong, because volume, weight and finish all move against you at the same time. This guide covers where each type of furniture is actually made, what each material costs and where it fails, the container maths that decides your landed price, and the compliance stack for the US, EU and UK. It is written from the buying desk, not from a supplier directory.

P1
The short answer

Why furniture, and why China
still wins on the maths.

Not because labour is cheap — that gap has been closing for a decade — but because the supply chain is dense.

In Foshan, hardware, foam, fabric, glass and stone tops are bought inside roughly a 50 km radius, which is what keeps a production cycle at 30–45 days instead of stretching while a factory waits on parts. It also means a well-planned day in Shunde or Nankang covers five or six factory audits; the same shortlist spread across provinces would take a week of travel.

Density is also what makes mixed containers work. A 40ft high-cube holds about 65–68 m³ and a packed guest-room set runs 3–5 m³, so one container usually carries goods from three or four factories — but only when those factories are close enough to truck to one loading point cheaply. That single constraint shapes every sourcing decision below.

The trade-off is that density hides variation. Two workshops on the same street can differ enormously in finishing quality, and the gap does not show up in a quotation. It shows up in a container, which is why inspection before the balance payment matters more here than in almost any other category.

The working rule is one primary cluster plus at most one satellite. Add a second cluster when a category is worth a container of its own, or when the primary cluster genuinely does not make it well — solid wood volume points to Nankang, task seating volume points to Anji. Spreading a mid-size order across four regions because each line looked marginally cheaper adds an inspection trip, a trucking leg and a coordination failure point per region, and on a two or three container project those costs eat whatever the line items saved.

Materials

Six material families.
And how each one fails.

Every line below is a specification that has to be written down before production. If it is only in an email, it is not in the order.

Solid wood

Specify · Species, moisture content, kiln-drying record, grade

Colour drift across one container, and timber that moves after it reaches a drier climate. Rubberwood, ash, oak and pine are the volume species; anything rarer needs the origin declared.

Engineered board & veneer

Specify · Board grade, formaldehyde class, veneer thickness, edge banding

The single most common place to quietly cut cost. Swelling at unsealed edges, delamination, and veneer that is too thin to sand back.

Metal

Specify · Gauge and wall thickness, weld finish, coating system

Thin-wall tube dents in transit, and powder coat flakes first at the welds. Ask for wall thickness in millimetres, not a photograph.

Rattan & cane

Specify · Natural or PE, weave density, frame material, grade consistency

Natural material needs consistent grading or the colour drifts piece to piece — the defect that shows up in one container, not one sample.

Upholstery

Specify · Foam density and ILD, fabric weight in gsm, rub count, leather cut

Foam density is the spec most often downgraded: 25D becomes 18D and the sofa feels different after ninety days. Put density, ILD and gsm in the spec sheet, not in an email.

Stone & glass

Specify · Thickness, edge finish, tempering, sealing

Untempered glass and unsealed stone top the damage list. Both are cheap to specify correctly and expensive to replace after arrival.

Cost structure

Six lines.
Only one is the unit price.

Comparing two quotations on unit price alone is the most common and most expensive mistake in this category.

MaterialBoard, timber, metal, fabric, leather, foam, stoneThe largest single line. Moves with commodity markets, so a quote held open for weeks is a quote that will be renegotiated.
Labour & finishingCutting, welding, sanding, upholstery, spray, veneer matchingWhere most of the gap between two quotes actually sits. Hand-matched veneer and multi-stage finishing are the difference between them.
Tooling & samplesJigs, moulds, prototype, corrected pre-production sampleOne-off cost, usually credited back once the bulk order is placed. Budget for two sample rounds, not one.
PackagingCartons, corner protection, crates, flat-pack engineeringUnder-invested by default. Damage claims on poorly packed furniture commonly run 3–8% of shipment value.
Consolidation & inland truckingFactory to warehouse to portFalls sharply when your factories share a cluster — which is the practical reason not to spread an order across four provinces.
Freight & dutyCharged on volume, not on valueA badly packed container can cost more per unit than a well-packed one, which is why packing is a cost line and not an afterthought.

A worked example that gets cited across the industry: a mid-range fabric sofa, 50 units, full container to the US West Coast. Bought direct, the unit price is around $180 FOB with the container filled to roughly 48 m³, which puts freight and surcharges near $88 per unit. Managed for packing and consolidation, the same sofa lands at about $166 with 65.5 m³ filled and freight near $58 per unit. The unit price moved 8%; the landed cost moved far more, and almost all of it came from volume.

Order mechanics

MOQ, samples
and realistic lead times.

These three numbers decide whether a project is viable long before price does.

01

Minimum order quantity

Set by the factory, not by us. Stocked items can ship from one carton. Custom upholstery usually starts at one 20GP or five to ten pieces per colour. Solid wood typically runs twenty to fifty pieces. We confirm the real figure with each factory before you commit.

02

Sampling

A real upholstered sofa prototype runs $300–900 and takes 15–25 days. Chairs and flat-pack pieces are $80–200. Budget two rounds: a prototype and a corrected pre-production sample. Keep a sealed approved sample as the reference production must match.

03

Lead time

30–45 days when components come from inside the cluster, 45–60 days when the spec needs custom veneer or imported timber. Add 7–20 days for sampling before that, and 3–7 days for consolidation, loading and export.

Packing and volume

Container maths.
Where margin actually hides.

Freight is charged on volume. Packing is therefore a cost line, not an afterthought.

The numbers

What fits

  • A 40ft high-cube holds about 65–68 m³ usable
  • A packed guest-room FF&E set runs 3–5 m³
  • One container usually carries three or four factories
  • Assembled furniture roughly doubles the shipped volume
  • Flat-pack halves freight and moves risk to the last mile
The failure

What it costs

  • Damage claims on poorly packed furniture run 3–8% of value
  • On a $40,000 shipment that is $2,000 — more than haggling 3% off the unit price saves
  • Corner protection and crates are cheap; a replacement is not
  • E-commerce parcels need ISTA-tested packaging, not export cartons
  • Loading should be supervised and photographed, every time

How to buy

Three routes.
Different risk, not different price.

All three can be correct. The question is which risks you want to carry yourself.

Factory direct

You hold the relationship, the verification and the risk

  • Best unit price on one high-volume item
  • You check the licence, capacity and export rights yourself
  • You arrange inspection, consolidation and the document set
  • Works well when you already know the factory and reorder the same SKU

Trading company

One counterparty, less visibility

  • Bundles several SKUs into one container and handles paperwork
  • Often cheaper to deal with than five separate factories
  • You may not learn which factory actually made your goods
  • Harder to hold quality accountable when something goes wrong

Buying agent — OZ

You keep the factory relationship, we carry the work

  • Three or more factories quoted on identical terms
  • Licences, capacity and export rights checked before quoting
  • Balance released only after a photo-backed AQL inspection
  • Everything consolidated into one container and one document set

Common questions

Before the first order.
Answered plainly.

These are the questions buyers ask us before they send a furniture brief.

What is a realistic MOQ for custom furniture from China?

Minimums are set by the factory, not by us. Stocked items can often ship from a single carton. Custom upholstery usually starts at one 20GP container or five to ten pieces per colour, because the cutting and fabric setup costs the same whether five or fifty are made. Solid wood runs higher, often twenty to fifty pieces. We confirm the real figure with each factory before you commit.

How long does a first furniture order take end to end?

Production runs 30–45 days when the factory can draw components from inside its own cluster, and 45–60 days when the specification needs custom veneer or imported timber. Add 7–20 days for sampling before that, and 3–7 days for consolidation, loading and export. Sea transit then depends on the lane: roughly 15–25 days to the US West Coast and 25–40 days to northern Europe.

Can I mix furniture from several factories in one container?

Yes, and for most project orders it is the only way the maths works. A 40ft high-cube holds about 65–68 m³, and a packed guest-room FF&E set runs 3–5 m³, so one container typically carries output from three or four factories. The catch is distance: factories far apart add a trucking leg and a separate inspection each. Consolidation is cheap inside one cluster and expensive across provinces.

Should I buy flat-pack or assembled?

Assembled furniture is safer to receive and roughly doubles the volume you pay to ship. Flat-pack halves the freight but moves the risk to the last mile, so it needs ISTA-tested packaging and clear assembly instructions. The usual split: assembled for hotel and project handover, flat-pack for e-commerce and anything a customer assembles at home.

Which Incoterm should a first order use?

FOB is the sensible default for container orders — we deliver to the port and load the vessel, and you control the ocean leg with your own forwarder. EXW looks cheaper but leaves you managing inland trucking and export declaration from abroad. CIF and DDP make sense once you want fewer moving parts and are happy with a longer quoting cycle.

How do you check that a factory is legitimate?

We check the business licence and its registered scope, confirm export rights, review the certifications your market needs, and visit or video-audit the production line. We report what we found including the weaknesses, because a factory that is excellent at upholstery is often quietly subcontracting your office chairs to somebody else.

Do I need my own inspector, or is yours enough?

Ours is enough for most orders. We run a mid-production check and a pre-shipment inspection against AQL 2.5, with AQL 4.0 accepted on some categories and tighter levels where your market requires it, and we send the photo report before you release the balance. If you have your own inspector we will coordinate with them rather than replace them.

Which certifications do I need for the US and the EU?

For the US: composite wood must meet CARB Phase 2 and TSCA Title VI, upholstery sold in California must meet TB 117-2013, office seating is usually specified to ANSI/BIFMA X5.1, and mattresses fall under 16 CFR 1632 and 1633. For the EU: the Deforestation Regulation requires geolocation due diligence on timber, and the General Product Safety Regulation requires traceability and an EU-based responsible person. Build the checklist per SKU and get test reports before production — retrofitting compliance onto a finished container is not possible.
